# Delhivery Last-Mile Delay Intelligence Pipeline

## How to run

From a fresh clone, run these commands from the repository root, in this order (Python 3.13; every script uses the fixed `random_state` values from the brief):

```
pip install -r requirements.txt
python generate_data.py
python part_a_clean_eda.py
python part_b_regression.py
python part_c_classification.py
python part_d_segments.py
python package_model.py
python predict_delay.py
```

`generate_data.py` writes `data/hubs.csv`, `data/raw_shipments.csv` and `data/new_shipments.csv`; Parts A to D write the CSV files in `outputs/`; `package_model.py` writes the four files in `models/`; and `predict_delay.py` writes `outputs/new_shipment_actions.csv`. `models/delay_model.pkl` is regenerated by running `python package_model.py`. The committed pickle loads reliably only on the scikit-learn version pinned in `requirements.txt` (1.9.1), and the grader re-runs `package_model.py` instead of loading the committed pickle when the installed versions differ. The cleaning and feature code lives once, in `src/shipment_prep.py`, and is shared by Part A and `predict_delay.py`.

## Data findings

All figures below are copied from the files in `outputs/` (written by `part_a_clean_eda.py`) and describe the 1,200 unique historical shipments in `data/modelling_table.csv`, of which 278 were delayed (overall delay rate 0.2317, `part_a_checks.csv`).

1. **Express is delayed most often.** In `part_a_delay_by_group.csv` the Express service has a delay rate of 0.4897 (119 of 243 parcels), against 0.2056 for Standard (132 of 642) and 0.0857 for Economy (27 of 315).
2. **Delay rate rises steeply with hub tier.** It goes from 0.04 for Tier 1 (16 of 400 parcels) to 0.215 for Tier 2 (86 of 400) and 0.44 for Tier 3 (176 of 400), so a Tier 3 parcel is delayed 11 times as often as a Tier 1 parcel.
3. **Delay rate rises with distance.** Across the distance bands in `part_a_delay_by_group.csv` it is 0.0219 for 0-200 km, 0.1368 for 201-400 km, 0.4364 for 401-600 km and 0.8523 for 601+ km (75 of 88 parcels delayed).
4. **Three columns are most strongly correlated with `delivery_hours`.** Ranked by absolute value in `part_a_correlations.csv` (excluding `delivery_hours` itself) they are `promised_hours` (0.8372), `distance_km` (0.4013) and `n_stops` (0.3247); all three are positive, so longer promises, longer lanes and longer rider routes all go with longer deliveries.
5. **`declared_value_inr` was log-transformed because it is right-skewed.** On the median-filled column the mean is INR 1,533.05 against a median of INR 1,121.50, whereas after the natural log the mean and median are almost equal (7.0277 and 7.0224, `part_a_checks.csv`), so a few very valuable parcels no longer dominate the feature.

## Model results (regression)

Figures come from `outputs/part_b_metrics.csv`, `part_b_tree_depth.csv`, `part_b_gb_learning_rate.csv` and `part_b_cv.csv`. The split is 840 / 180 / 180 rows (train / validation / test); the scaler was fitted on the training rows only (`train_scaled_max_abs_mean` is 0.0, `test_scaled_max_abs_mean` is 0.2187 in `part_b_checks.csv`).

- **Baseline.** Predicting the training mean for every validation parcel gives R² = -0.0212 and RMSE = 18.8552 hours. An R² of about zero means the mean explains none of the variation in delivery hours, so any useful model has to beat this.
- **Validation winner.** Linear regression has the lowest validation RMSE, 4.8912 hours (R² 0.9313), narrowly ahead of gradient boosting at 4.9165 hours (R² 0.9306), then random forest at 5.468 hours and the decision tree at 6.2796 hours. Scored once on the test rows, linear regression gives RMSE 4.5235 hours and R² 0.9493.
- **Tree depth (bias versus variance).** Depths 1, 2 and 3 are high-bias: train and validation errors are both high (depth 1: train RMSE 11.6334, validation RMSE 12.1324) and the gap between them is small (0.4990, 1.0427 and 1.1738 hours). Depths 12 and unlimited are high-variance: the training error collapses (1.3613 and 0.0) while the validation error is back up at 7.4173 and 7.789, a gap of 6.056 and 7.789 hours; depth 8 is already overfitting (gap 3.5025). Depth 6 has the lowest validation RMSE, 6.2796 hours (gap 1.5682), and is the depth used for the tree candidate.
- **Learning rate.** A learning rate of 0.1 is best (validation RMSE 4.9165, R² 0.9306). At 0.01 the R² is only 0.7127 because each of the 100 trees corrects the residuals by just 1% of its step, so the ensemble has not had time to fit the signal (underfitting from too slow a rate). At 1.0 the R² drops to 0.844 (RMSE 7.3701) because every tree's full correction is applied, so each step overshoots and the ensemble chases noise in the residuals.
- **Cross-validation disagrees with the validation ranking.** On the 1,020 train-plus-validation rows (one shared 5-fold `KFold`), gradient boosting has a mean R² of 0.9352 (std 0.0044) against 0.9261 (std 0.0048) for linear regression, the reverse of the validation order. **Decision: ship gradient boosting** (`GradientBoostingRegressor`, 100 trees, learning rate 0.1). The validation gap is only 0.0253 hours of RMSE on 180 parcels, while the cross-validated comparison uses all 1,020 rows over five folds and is the more stable estimate. The 0.0091 R² advantage is about two fold standard deviations, so it is modest evidence rather than proof, and linear regression remains a close and simpler alternative. The test rows were scored once, for the validation winner (linear regression, following the rule that the final model has the lowest validation RMSE), so gradient boosting has no test score of its own; its evidence is the validation RMSE of 4.9165 hours and the cross-validated R² of 0.9352.

## Model results (classification)

Figures come from `outputs/part_c_models.csv`, `part_c_feature_scores.csv`, `part_c_selectk.csv`, `part_c_tuning.csv`, `part_c_thresholds.csv`, `part_c_final_test.csv` and `part_c_final_config.csv`. The stratified split is 840 / 180 / 180 rows with 195 / 42 / 41 delayed parcels.

- **Blind baseline.** Predicting "not delayed" for every parcel scores an accuracy of 0.7667 on the validation rows (TN 138, FN 42), yet it catches none of the 42 delayed parcels (recall 0.0). Because about 23% of parcels are delayed, accuracy rewards ignoring the very parcels operations needs to find, so it is useless as a model; the trained models are judged on recall, precision and F1 instead.
- **Noise columns.** The five bookkeeping columns together hold 0.2245 of the default forest's feature importance (`rf_importance` summed over `is_noise` in `part_c_feature_scores.csv`), even though they carry no signal; a forest spends splits on them because they let it fit the training rows. Their F-scores are all below 5 (the largest is `label_print_batch` at 4.5847), and yes, all five fall outside the top 7 by F-score: they rank 8, 12, 14, 16 and 17, while the seven real columns (`distance_km`, `hub_tier`, `hub_daily_capacity`, `n_stops`, `promised_hours`, `rain_flag`, `sale_event_flag`) rank 1 to 7 and the smallest of their F-scores is 36.7281.
- **Selecting k.** Validation F1 is 0.6988 at k = 5, 0.8333 at k = 7, 0.7857 at k = 10, 0.8049 at k = 12 and 0.7848 at k = 17. Keeping the best 7 columns beats using all 17 by 0.0485, and k = 5 is worse because it drops `rain_flag` and `sale_event_flag`, which do carry signal. This is one validation split of 42 delayed parcels, so differences of a few points are partly luck, but it supports k = 7, used from here on.
- **Did tuning help?** No. The untuned forest on the same 7 columns has a validation F1 of 0.8333, ahead of the grid search at 0.775 and the randomized search at 0.7848, so `part_c_final_config.csv` records `default_forest_same_7_features`. The two searches differ by only 0.0047 in cross-validated accuracy (0.8952 against 0.8905), and a gap of this size on a 180-row validation set, with 42 delayed parcels, is within noise; that is a warning against tuning to one small validation set rather than proof that the defaults are better.
- **Alert threshold.** Under the operations rule (the highest threshold whose validation recall is at least 0.80), the chosen threshold is 0.5. At 0.5 the validation precision is 0.7955 and recall 0.8333 (7 delayed parcels missed). Moving up to 0.6 would raise precision by 0.0934 to 0.8889 but cut recall by 0.0714 to 0.7619 (10 missed), below the target, while moving down to 0.4 lifts recall to 0.881 at a precision of 0.74.
- **Test result.** At threshold 0.5 the final model scores a test precision of 0.6889, recall of 0.7561 and F1 of 0.7209 (TP 31, FP 14, FN 10). Test recall is 0.0772 below the validation recall of 0.8333 and under the 0.80 target. The test set holds only 41 delayed parcels, so one parcel moves recall by about 0.024 and the gap amounts to roughly three parcels; the threshold was fitted on the validation rows, so the validation recall is optimistic and the test figure is the honest, but noisy, estimate.

## Operations recommendation

Figures come from `outputs/hub_profile.csv`, `segment_profile.csv`, `hub_segments.csv`, `tier_vs_segment_mismatch.csv`, `part_d_separation.csv`, `part_c_final_test.csv` and `new_shipment_actions.csv`.

- **Hubs to put on the Escalate playbook.** The Escalate segment has 9 hubs and a mean hub delay rate of 0.46 (`segment_profile.csv`): Guwahati (HUB19, 0.62), Agartala (HUB24, 0.54), Raipur (HUB20, 0.48), Nagpur (HUB13, 0.46), Patna (HUB17, 0.46), Siliguri (HUB23, 0.44), Bhubaneswar (HUB21, 0.40), Ranchi (HUB18, 0.38) and Kochi (HUB15, 0.36). By comparison the Watch segment (8 hubs) has a mean delay rate of 0.155 and the Standard segment (7 hubs) 0.0257.
- **Tier labels that mislead.** Four hubs behave unlike their tier (`tier_vs_segment_mismatch.csv`): Nagpur (HUB13) and Kochi (HUB15) are labelled Tier 2 but sit in Escalate, with delay rates of 0.46 and 0.36, while Kolkata (HUB07, Tier 1) and Dehradun (HUB22, Tier 3) sit in Watch, with delay rates of 0.14 and 0.2. The tier label should therefore be used as a starting point only: Nagpur and Kochi should be run on the Escalate playbook, Kolkata should not be treated as a Standard hub, and Dehradun can follow the lighter Watch playbook until its numbers move.
- **Alert threshold.** Parcels are flagged when the delay probability is at least 0.5, the highest threshold that keeps validation recall at 0.80 or above. On the untouched test rows (`part_c_final_test.csv`) the precision of 0.6889 means that about 6.9 of every 10 flagged parcels are really delayed, and the recall of 0.7561 means that about 7.6 of every 10 delayed parcels are caught.
- **The 48 new parcels.** `new_shipment_actions.csv` flags 8 of the 48 parcels, and 6 of those 8 belong to Escalate hubs and need a hub-manager escalation; the other 2 get a proactive customer call.
- **Why K = 3.** Three playbooks is fixed by operations, and K = 3 already separates the hubs well: the across-to-within distance ratio rises from 2.894 at K = 2 to 5.3928 at K = 3. It keeps rising at K = 4 (6.3047) and K = 5 (6.8866), but those solutions contain clusters of 4 hubs (sizes 8-7-5-4) and of 3 and 2 hubs (sizes 8-7-4-3-2), too small to carry a playbook of their own, so the higher ratio is not worth it.

## Production plan

**(a) Production-readiness characteristics mapped to this repository.**

| Characteristic | Where it is in the repository |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Held-out evidence in `outputs/part_c_final_test.csv` (precision 0.6889, recall 0.7561) and `outputs/part_b_metrics.csv`. |
| Reproducibility | Fixed `random_state` values, versions pinned in `requirements.txt`, `generate_data.py` committed verbatim, and `python package_model.py` regenerating `models/delay_model.pkl`; two runs of `predict_delay.py` give byte-identical output. |
| Consistency | Training and prediction both call `clean_shipments` and `build_features` from `src/shipment_prep.py`, and `models/impute_values.csv` and `models/feature_order.csv` carry the fill-in medians and the 17-column order into scoring. |
| Maintainability | One script per Part, a single shared module with the pinned `FEATURES` and `NOISE_COLUMNS` constants, and a README that lists the run order. |
| Reliability | `clean_shipments` handles duplicates, mixed-case service types, the 999 kg sentinel, and missing values, so `data/new_shipments.csv` scores without error. |
| Monitorability | `outputs/new_shipment_actions.csv` records the probability, flag, segment and action for every parcel, and `outputs/part_a_checks.csv` gives the historical baselines to compare against. |

**(b) Quantities to monitor, with numeric triggers.**

- *Weekly delayed share of delivered parcels.* The historical delay rate is 0.2317 (`part_a_checks.csv`); investigate if a normal week moves outside 0.18 to 0.28 for two weeks in a row.
- *Weekly share of parcels flagged.* On the test rows the model flags 45 of 180 parcels (0.25); investigate if the weekly flagged share leaves 0.15 to 0.35.
- *Recall on parcels whose outcome is now known.* Measured over a rolling four weeks with at least 40 delayed parcels, it should stay near the test recall of 0.7561; retrain if it falls below 0.65.
- *Missing inputs.* Historically 36 of 1,200 parcels (0.03) lacked `distance_km`; investigate if the missing rate of any filled column exceeds 0.06.

**(c) Retraining and the sale window.** Retrain every quarter on all parcels with a known outcome, and immediately if the recall trigger fires in two consecutive checks. A retrain repeats Part C on a new validation set, including the threshold rule (highest threshold with recall of at least 0.80), re-runs Part D to refresh the hub segments, and then re-packages the model. The 15-28 September sale window (`sale_event_flag`) should be judged against the previous sale window or another comparable event, never against ordinary weeks, because delays are expected to be higher during a sale and a plain weekly comparison would raise false alarms; ordinary weeks should be compared with ordinary weeks. Data drift here would look like a shift in the inputs (for example longer average `distance_km`, more COD parcels, or a changed service mix), a shift in the delay rate for the same inputs, or a hub moving to a different operating profile, as Nagpur, Kochi, Kolkata and Dehradun already have relative to their tier.

## Limitations

- **Synthetic data.** Every row comes from `generate_data.py`, not from Delhivery, so the numbers show the method working and are not a forecast of real operations.
- **Few delayed parcels in the test set.** The 180 test parcels contain only 41 delayed ones, so one parcel moves recall by about 0.024 and the test precision, recall and F1 carry wide uncertainty.
- **Threshold tuned on one validation split.** The threshold of 0.5 was chosen on 180 validation rows with 42 delayed parcels, so the validation recall of 0.8333 is optimistic, as the lower test recall of 0.7561 shows.
- **Random split rather than a time split.** The parcels span three months but are split at random, so the model has not been tested on genuinely later dispatches, which is how it will be used.
- **Small hub sample.** Each hub has only 50 parcels and there are 24 hubs, so a hub's segment can change with a few parcels, and the K-Means result with K = 3 is fixed by the business rather than found from the data.
